## Video 5: Evolution of Language Models: From Traditional to Transformers

Language models have existed way before ChatGPT. With this, let me welcome you to our exploration of the evolution of large language models. You may be surprised to hear that GPT was not the first in this series. So, in this lecture, we'll trace the journey from traditional language models to the revolutionary transformer architecture, understanding how each step has brought us closer to much more sophisticated AI language understanding.

Traditional language models, like N-gram models, but based on statistical probability of word sequences. For example, Bi-gram model would predict the next word based on the probability of it following the current word. While this was groundbreaking at the time, these models had significant limitations right. They were constrained by fixed context windows, typically looking at only a few words at a time. They also suffered from data sparsity, as many possible word combinations were never seen in the training data. And they also struggled understanding long range dependencies in broader context in text. So, these limitations led researchers to seek much more sophisticated approaches to language modelling.

The advent of neural network-based models marked a significantly forward here. So first, the feedforward neural networks improved on N-gram models by learning more complex relationships between words. Then we have the (RNNs) that Recurrent Neural Networks, they introduced the ability to process sequence of words, maintaining a form of memory about previous inputs.

And lastly, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, this is a type of RNN, further improved the ability to capture long-term dependencies on text. These models significantly outperformed traditional statistic models, showing an improved understanding of language context and semantics. However, they still struggled with very long sentences and were computationally intensive train, especially when it comes to large data sets.

So, the introduction of the attention mechanism was a key pivotal moment in the evolution of language models. Attention allows a model to focus on different parts of the input when producing each part of the output. So, this addressed several key limitations of previous models. First, it allowed for much better handling of long-range dependencies, as the model could "attend" to relevant parts of the input regardless of their position. Second, it provided a way to parallelise computation, making training much more efficient. Third, it improved the interpretability of models, as we could analyse which part of the input the model was focusing on for each output. The attention mechanism was first introduced in the context of neural machine translation but quickly provided value across various natural language processing tasks.

The transformer architecture, introduced in the year 2017 in a paper called "Attention is All You Need," was a revolutionary step in language modelling. So, the key aspects of the transformers include that they rely entirely on the attention mechanism, doing away with recurrence, doing away with convolutions, that were used in previous models. They also use self-attention, which allows the model to attend to different positions of the input sequence for each element of the sequence. They incorporate positional encoding to maintain information about the order of words in the sequence. And lastly, they enable highly parallelised processing, making them much more efficient to train on large datasets. Transformers achieved state-of-the-art performance on various NLP tasks and formed the basis for models like BERT, GPT, and their successes, which have pushed the boundaries of what's possible in natural language processing.

The impact of transformers on language models has been profound. They've enabled the development of extremely large, of extremely powerful models like GPT-3, which can perform a wide range of language tasks with remarkable proficiency. They've facilitated transfer learning in NLP, where models pre-trained on large corpora can be fine-tuned for specific tasks with relatively little task-specific data. They've also opened up entirely new possibilities in few-shot and zero-shot learning, where models can perform tasks with very few or even no specific examples. So, these advancements have dramatically expanded the capabilities and applications of language models in various domains.

The evolution of language models from traditional statistical approaches to transformers represents a remarkable journey in AI. As we look for the future, research continues into more efficient architectures, better handling of long sequences, and has also improved few-shot learning capabilities. The field of language modelling remains dynamic and full of potential for further breakthroughs.

## Video 8: Fine-Tuning LLMs

Let us now explore how fine-tuning of Large Language Models works and what's involved here. So, in this lecture, we'll look at the process of adopting pre-trained models to specific tasks, to specific domains where they can unlock their full potential for specialised applications.

Fine-tuning is the process of taking a pre-trained language model and further training it on a specific dataset or for a particular task. Unlike training from scratch, which requires vast amounts of data and computational resources, fine-tuning leverages the general knowledge of a pre-trained model and adapts it to a specific domain or to a specific task. This is much more efficient, and this allows for creating specialised models with relatively small data sets and less computational power.

Fine-tuning is particularly beneficial when you have pre-trained models who struggle with domain specific language or jargon. They're also extremely useful when you need to perform a task that is specialised, and a generic model can't handle this really well. Imagine a scenario, where you want to improve performance on a specific type of input or output. Or a scenario where you need to adapt the model style or tone for a very particular use case. Let's look at an example, a legal firm might fine-tune an LLM on legal documents to improve its performance in legal research and document analysis tasks.

So, this fine-tuning process typically involves these steps. First, you need to do a data preparation: You collect and clean a data set specific to your task. You must ensure its representative and high quality. Then you choose a pre-trained model: You basically select a base model that is suitable for your task and for the computational resources that you have. Hyper perimeter selection: Decide on the learning rate, batch size, the number of epochs, etc. These can significantly impact the results. The actual training itself: Where you fine-tune the model on your data set, often using techniques like gradual unfreezing or discriminative fine-tuning. The next step is evaluation: Where you assess the model's performance on a held-out test set. And ultimately iteration: Where you, based on these results, you adjust the hyper parameters or data and repeat the process if it's really necessary. The key success for fine-tuning is often the quality of the dataset and the careful selection of the hyperparameters.

Several advanced techniques can enhance the fine-tuning process even more. So here we talk about gradual unfreezing: Where you start by fine-tuning only the last layer, then gradually unfreeze and train earlier layers. This helps prevent catastrophic forgetting of general knowledge for example. Discriminative fine-tuning: Here we apply different learning rates to different layers of the model. Typically, higher learning rates for later layers and lower rates for earlier layers. We've got layer wise learning rate decay: Decrease the learning rate for layers close to the input. This preserves low level features while allowing task specific adaptations in higher layers. So, these techniques help balance the preservation of general knowledge with adaptation to specific tasks, often leading to much better performance and stability in the fine-tuned model.

So, while fine-tuning a language model can be powerful, it comes with a couple of challenges. Overfitting is one of them, the model may become too specialised, performing well on the training data, but poorly on new unseen data.

Catastrophic forgetting: The model might lose its general capabilities as it specialises, forgetting useful knowledge from previous training. Ethical considerations: Fine-tuning can potentially amplify biases present in the specialised dataset. So, it's crucial to carefully curate your training data and monitor the model's output for unintended biases. To address these challenges, you can use techniques like early stopping, careful data curation and continuous evaluation on diverse data sets.

So now consider a case where a generic LLM was fine-tuned on a data set of medical literature and patient records.
